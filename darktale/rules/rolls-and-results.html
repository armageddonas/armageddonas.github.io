Rolls and results

Attribute plus Skill in d6. Every 5 or 6 succeeds, and the stakes die adds the swing.

  • Pool: Attribute + Skill in d6. Every 5 or 6 is a success.
  • Untrained skills: When you roll a skill you have no dots in, take −1d for a Physical or Social skill, or −3d for a Mental skill.
  • Chance die: If penalties reduce your pool to 0 dice or fewer, roll a single d6 instead. Only a 6 is a success. A 1 is a Complication with no success.
  • Rerolls: Each die can be rerolled only once per roll.
  • Exploding dice: When a rule says 6s explode, each 6 adds another die to the roll, which can explode too. Exploding dice are new dice, not rerolls. The stakes die and chance dice never explode.
  • Contested rolls: Both sides roll. The side with more successes wins; the margin is the net successes. Ties go to the side that acts: the attacker, or whoever is trying to do something to the other. A tie is a win with 0 net successes, so an attack that ties is a hit with 0 net successes. The side that acts still needs at least 1 success: if neither side rolls a success, the action fails.
  • Success with Opportunity: Any roll with 5 or more successes is a success with Opportunity. The stakes die can also give an Opportunity (below), even on a failed roll. Talents that "expand the Opportunity range" lower this threshold by 1 per step (to 4+, then 3+). The threshold never goes below 3.
  • Stakes die: When a character raises the stakes, they roll an extra d6 of a different colour along with their pool:
    • 5–6: an Opportunity, but no success.
    • 1–2: a Complication, which also counts as a success.
    • 3–4: nothing.

    Overall, the stakes die succeeds as often as a normal die; what it adds is the swing. An Opportunity from the stakes die applies even if the roll fails: you don't get what you wanted, but you gain something else.

  • Raising the stakes: The GM raises the stakes on a roll when the outcome could change the situation in a big way: a desperate leap, a lie to someone powerful, a blow that could end a fight. Once per day, a player can also choose to raise the stakes on their own roll, before rolling. Talents and effects can add more uses. A roll never has more than one stakes die.
  • Opportunities and Complications: On an Opportunity, the player chooses a benefit that fits the roll (an extra effect, a useful detail, a better position), and the GM approves it. On a Complication, the GM adds a setback that fits the roll (a lost item, an alerted guard, a slip). A Complication never cancels a success. A roll can show both. Either can be spent on an event track instead.
  • Common Opportunities and Complications: when nothing else says what a result does (a weapon trait, Dodge, a talent, an event track), choose one of these, or another that fits the roll:
    • Opportunity: an ally gets +1d on their next roll toward the same goal, before the end of their next turn; you become Determined; the target takes −1d on its next roll, before the end of its next turn; fill a box on a boon event track; or a story benefit (a useful detail, a better position, time or materials saved).
    • Complication: an ally takes −1d on their next roll; you take 1 Stress; you suffer a fitting condition (Prone, Slowed or Disoriented) until the end of your next turn; fill a box on a threat event track; or a story setback (a lost item, an alerted guard, lost time).
  • Teamwork: Before a character rolls, a helper can roll a fitting Attribute + Skill (in combat, this costs the helper 1 action). With at least 1 success, the main roll gets +1d; on an Opportunity, +2d. If the helper's roll shows a Complication, the main roll suffers it too. Helpers give at most +2d to a single roll in total.
  • Group actions: when the whole group does the same thing together and one result decides it for everyone (sneaking past a guard post, climbing a wall, holding a line), one character leads it. Everyone involved rolls, the leader included, and the best roll counts for the whole group, with its own Opportunity or Complication; ties go to the leader's roll if it's one of them. The leader takes 1 Stress for each roll that failed (no successes). Each participant can push themselves on their own roll. Teamwork is for helping one character's roll; a group action is for when everyone's roll is at stake.
  • Specialties: +1d or more when one applies (see Specialties).
  • Fixed difficulty: When there's no opposing roll, the roll needs a number of successes: 2 is a hard task, 3 is a very hard one.

Modifiers

Situational bonuses and penalties to dice pools. They add together; if they bring a pool to 0 dice or fewer, roll a chance die.

  • Advantage / disadvantage: +1d / −1d, for anything that helps or hinders a roll and has no rule of its own.

Light

Rolls that rely on sight take −1d in dim light and −3d in darkness. In total darkness (no light at all, or unnatural darkness a setting defines), you're effectively Blinded: rolls that rely on sight use a chance die.

Cover and concealment

  • Cover: Something solid blocks part of you. Ranged attacks against you take −1d. With full cover, you can't be targeted.
  • Obscured: Smoke, fog or shadow makes you Obscured: rolls to see or target you take −1d, and you can try to hide with Stealth. Deeper darkness uses the Light rules.

Position

  • Prone: melee attacks against you get +1d; ranged attacks against you get −1d.
  • Difficult terrain: −1d to Physical rolls, including attacks and defense rolls, while you stand in it (see Terrain and forced movement).
  • Unstable footing: on a rocking boat, a moving cart or train roof, or a narrow ledge, ranged and thrown attacks take −1d. If the footing is also difficult terrain, the penalties don't stack.
  • Bracing: the Brace action gives +1d to your defense rolls while you're in cover.

Contested pools

When an effect is "contested by" a pool, the target rolls the one that fits:

AgainstContested by
Feints, tricks, lies, mental attacksIntelligence + Resolve
Pressure, taunts, intimidation, fearWits + Composure
Physical effects that aren't attacks (shoves, poisons, traps)Dexterity + Stamina
Noticing something hidden (a sneaking character, a concealed trap)Wits + Composure

Extended actions

An extended action is a task that takes many rolls over time: researching in an archive, repairing a machine, forging papers, tracking someone across open country.

The GM sets:

  • Pool: the Attribute + Skill that fits, as usual.
  • Target: the total successes needed.
  • Interval: how long each roll takes (minutes, hours or days).
TaskTargetRolls with a 6-die pool
Quick3about 2
Standard6about 3
Long10about 5
Major15about 8
  • Rolling: each roll adds its successes to the total. When the total reaches the target, the task is done.
  • Opportunity: choose a benefit as usual, such as finishing this interval in half the time, saving materials, or a useful discovery. An Opportunity never adds successes to the total.
  • Complication: the GM adds a setback that fits: lose 1 success already gained, spend extra time or materials, or draw attention.
  • No successes: take 1 Stress to press on, or the attempt fails. Trying again usually needs a new approach, new tools or new information. (During downtime, no Stress is paid: the work stops until a later Project action.)
  • Pushing, the stakes die and specialties work on each roll as normal, except that downtime rolls can't be pushed.
  • Teamwork works on each roll. If the GM agrees the task can be split (searching an archive, but not forging one document), several characters can instead work in parallel: each rolls their own pool every interval, and all their successes count toward the target.
  • Deadlines: the GM can set one ("before nightfall"). If the target isn't reached in time, the task fails or only partly succeeds.
  • Event tracks can run alongside an extended action, to show a danger closing in or help coming together (see Event tracks).
  • Crafting and repair are extended actions with their own targets and upgrades (see Crafting).
  • Contested extended actions chases, races, a long debate both sides roll each interval, and the first to reach the target wins. Ties go to the side that started it (the pursuer, the challenger).

Event tracks

An event track puts something that's coming on the table: reinforcements on the way, an alarm being raised, a plan coming together, a fire spreading. The GM announces the event and draws its track, a row of 2–6 boxes, where the players can see it. When the last box fills, the event happens.

  • Threat tracks are events the characters want to stop. The GM can spend a Complication on a character's roll to fill a box, instead of adding its usual setback. An NPC's Opportunity can fill one the same way.
  • Boon tracks are events the characters want. A player can spend an Opportunity on their roll to fill a box, instead of taking its usual benefit. An NPC's Complication can fill one the same way.
  • Triggers: a track can also name things that fill it (a guard sees you, each hour that passes, each interval of the repair) or clear a box (a convincing lie to a suspicious guard). The GM says them when the track goes up. They apply whatever the dice show.
  • Only rolls that bear on the event can fill its track: an alarm in the house fills from rolls inside the house, not from a brawl in the street outside.
  • One box per result: an Opportunity or a Complication fills one box. A roll that shows both can fill a box on a boon track and a box on a threat track.
  • Stages: a track can name smaller effects at a box before the last ("at 2, the guards grow uneasy").
  • Racing tracks: a threat track and a boon track can run against each other. Whichever fills first decides how the scene turns.
  • When it fills: the event happens and the track ends, unless it says it starts again.
  • Size: about the number of Complications the scene can take: 2–3 for a short scene, 4 for a scene of about a dozen rolls, 6 for a long one.
  • Raising the stakes: Complications come only from the stakes die and from chance dice, so a threat track fills only when the stakes are raised. While one is up, the GM raises the stakes on rolls where its danger is actually present (sneaking past a guard, cracking the safe), not on every roll. With the stakes raised, a box fills about once every three rolls.

Flashbacks

The story doesn't stop for planning. When a moment calls for something your character could have done earlier, a player can call a flashback: play out a short scene from the past, then return to the present with its result. Flashbacks happen outside the round and take no time now.

  • Cost: the GM sets it by how likely the preparation is.
PreparationStressExample
Simple or complex1You bribed the doorman to leave the side gate unlatched
Elaborate or unlikely2You planted a forged letter in the magistrate's desk last week
  • Obvious things aren't flashbacks. What any competent character would have done (checked the guard rotation, brought a lantern) the GM simply grants, as part of playing someone who knows their job.
  • Rolling: if the preparation was uncertain, roll it now as it would have been rolled then, with all the usual rules (pushing, teamwork, the stakes die). On a failure, it didn't work, or it worked with a catch the GM adds now.
  • Limits: a flashback can't contradict what's already happened in play, and it has to have been possible: you had the time, the place and the means. It changes what happens next, not what just happened: it can't undo a hit already landed or a guard who already saw you.
  • Gear: a flashback can leave things in the scene (a rope tied off on the roof, a pistol hidden in the coatroom) as long as you pay their price as usual. It can't put an item in your hands or your pack that you didn't carry.
